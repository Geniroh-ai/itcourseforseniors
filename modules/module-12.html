Your First Conversation with an AI

Opening a conversation with ChatGPT, Gemini, or Claude for the first time can feel a bit like ringing someone you haven't met before. What do you say? Where do you start?

The answer is simple: just say what you need, in natural language. There's no special code or command. You type (or speak) as if you were talking to a very knowledgeable, patient, and helpful person.

💻 How to Get Started (step by step)

1

Go to the AI's website or open its app

For ChatGPT: chat.openai.com · For Gemini: gemini.google.com · For Claude: claude.ai · On Windows: search for "Copilot" in the taskbar

2

Create a free account (first time only)

You'll need to provide an email address and create a password. This takes about 2 minutes and is completely free.

3

Click the text box and type your question or request

Just like typing an email or a search. Type what you need, then press Enter or click the send button (▶).

4

Read the response and continue the conversation

If the answer isn't quite what you needed, ask a follow-up question. Say "Can you explain that more simply?" or "Give me a shorter version" — it will adapt.

💡

Your First Prompt Idea

If you're not sure what to ask first, try this: "Hello! I'm new to this. Can you explain what you can help me with and give me 5 examples of useful things I could ask you?" — it's a great way to discover what's possible.

Writing Better Prompts

A "prompt" is simply what you type to the AI. The quality of your prompt affects the quality of the answer. You don't need to be perfect — the AI is very forgiving — but a few simple habits produce much better results:

❌

Vague Prompt

"Tell me about my health" — too broad. The AI doesn't know what aspect you mean or what to focus on.

✅

Clear Prompt

"I take medication for high blood pressure. Can you explain in simple terms what that condition is and what lifestyle changes are commonly recommended?" — specific, context-rich, clear request.

Prompt Tips That Work

  • Give context — "I'm 68 years old and…" / "I'm writing a letter to my council about…" tells the AI who you are and what situation you're in
  • State the format you want — "Give me a bullet-point list" / "Write this as a formal letter" / "Explain it in a short paragraph"
  • Say if you want it simpler — "Explain this as if I'm not technical" or "Use plain English"
  • Ask follow-up questions — the conversation continues; each message adds context
  • Say if it didn't work — "That wasn't quite what I meant — can you try again, but this time…"

Practical Uses — Real Examples for Your Life

Here are specific, practical ways people in the 60+ age group are already using AI chatbots:

✍️ Writing Help

  • "Can you help me write a letter to my insurance company disputing a claim? Here are the details: [your details]"
  • "Please proofread and improve this email I've written to my landlord: [paste your email]"
  • "Can you write a thank-you note to my neighbour who looked after my cat while I was away?"
  • "Help me write a birthday message for my granddaughter who loves horses and just turned 10"

🏥 Understanding Health Information

  • "My doctor mentioned 'atrial fibrillation.' Can you explain what this is in plain English?"
  • "I've been prescribed metformin. What is it for and what side effects should I know about?" (Remember to always confirm medication questions with your pharmacist or GP)
  • "Can you help me understand what this section of my hospital discharge letter means? [paste the text]"
⚕️

Health Information — Important Caution

AI is very useful for understanding health concepts and preparing for conversations with your doctor. But never use it to diagnose yourself or decide on treatment. Always consult your GP, pharmacist, or the NHS (NHS 111) for actual medical decisions. Use AI to become a better-informed patient — not as a substitute for professional care.

🧳 Planning and Exploring

  • "I'm planning a 5-day trip to the Lake District in September for two people in their 70s who enjoy gentle walks and good food. Can you suggest an itinerary?"
  • "What are the best gardens to visit within an hour of Bath?"
  • "Can you suggest some easy crossword-style puzzles or word games I could play with my grandchildren aged 7 and 10?"

📚 Learning and Curiosity

  • "Can you explain the main events of the Cold War in a way that would interest someone who lived through it?"
  • "I've been watching a documentary about ancient Rome. What are the most fascinating facts about daily life in the Roman Empire?"
  • "Can you recommend some novels similar in style to Agatha Christie?"

💡 Everyday Problem-Solving

  • "My dishwasher is leaving white marks on glasses. What are the common causes and how do I fix it?"
  • "Can you give me a recipe for a lemon drizzle cake that serves 8, without too much sugar?"
  • "I'm getting a lot of unsolicited calls. What are the best ways to reduce them in the UK?"

When to Use AI — and When Not To

✅

Great Uses for AI

Writing and editing. Understanding complex documents. Answering general questions. Planning and ideas. Learning new topics. Summarising text. Translation. Creative projects.

⚠️

Be Careful or Avoid

Medical diagnosis or treatment decisions. Legal advice (use as background, then see a solicitor). Financial decisions (see an IFA). Real-time information (news, train times). Emotional crises (speak to a person).

🎯

The "Research Assistant" Rule

Think of AI as a brilliant research assistant who has read millions of books and can explain anything — but who should always be verified for facts, and who should never replace the final judgement of a qualified professional. Use it to understand, prepare, and explore. Use professionals to decide and act.

Staying Curious and in Control

AI is evolving rapidly. What it can do this year is significantly more than last year, and next year will bring further changes. Here's how to stay confident and in control as things develop:

1

Just Try It

The best way to learn is to start. Open ChatGPT or Gemini and ask something you're genuinely curious about. There's nothing to break, nothing to lose. You can always start a new conversation.

2

Stay Sceptical

Approach AI responses as you would advice from a knowledgeable friend — useful, often right, but always worth double-checking for anything important. You are the decision-maker; the AI is the assistant.

3

Keep Learning

Good sources for staying informed include BBC News (excellent technology coverage), Which? magazine (practical consumer-focused AI guides), and Age UK (which provides resources for older adults navigating technology).

4

Share and Discuss

Talk to family, friends, and peers about what you're discovering. These conversations are valuable — both for learning and for helping others feel less anxious about new technology.

5

Remember: You Are in Control

AI works for you. You decide what to ask, what to do with the answers, and when to stop using it. Technology serves people — not the other way around. Your experience, judgement, and values matter more than any algorithm.
