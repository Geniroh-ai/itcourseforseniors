Staying Safe Online

The internet is mostly wonderful — but like any place, it helps to know what to watch out for. You'll be confident and safe in no time.

🟡 Intermediate ⏱ 15 minutes 📖 Reading

What You'll Learn

  • How to create strong, memorable passwords
  • What phishing and online scams look like — and how to spot them
  • How to protect your personal information online
  • Safe online shopping — what to look for
  • What to do if something goes wrong

Passwords — Your First Line of Defence

A password is like the lock on your front door. A weak password is like a flimsy lock that's easy to pick. A strong password protects your accounts from people who might try to get in uninvited.

What Makes a Password Strong?

  • At least 12 characters long — the longer the better
  • Uses a mix of capital letters, lowercase letters, numbers, and symbols
  • Is not an obvious word like your name, birthdate, or "password"
  • Is different for each website or account you use
🔑

A Good Trick: Use a Passphrase

Instead of a single word, pick three or four random words and join them together: BlueSunflowerDoor42 — it's long, memorable, and very hard to guess. Think of four items on your desk right now and combine them: "LampMugBookPen" is much stronger than "password123".

Password Do's and Don'ts

✅

DO

Use different passwords for each important account. Use a password manager app (like Dashlane or 1Password) to remember them. Enable "two-factor authentication" where offered.

❌

DON'T

Use your name, birthday, or "123456" as a password. Write passwords on sticky notes near your computer. Share passwords with anyone, even someone claiming to be tech support.

📱

Two-Factor Authentication (2FA)

This is an extra security step where, after you enter your password, the website sends a short code to your phone. You type in that code to prove it's really you. It's like a bouncer who checks your ID even after seeing your membership card. It's very effective and well worth enabling on your email and banking accounts.

Recognising Scams and Phishing

Phishing (pronounced "fishing") is when scammers send fake emails, texts, or phone calls pretending to be a trusted organisation — your bank, the government, or a delivery company — to trick you into revealing personal information or clicking a dangerous link.

It's called "phishing" because scammers are "fishing" for victims — casting out many fake messages hoping someone will take the bait.

Common Scam Types

!

"Your bank account has been compromised"

An urgent email or text claims your bank account is in danger and you must click a link immediately. Real banks never ask for passwords or card details by email or text.

!

Fake parcel delivery notifications

"Your parcel couldn't be delivered — click here to reschedule." Often asks for a small payment or personal details. If you're not expecting a parcel, treat this with great suspicion.

!

HMRC / Tax refund scams

"You're owed a tax rebate — click here to claim it." The government never contacts you for refunds this way.

!

Tech support calls

Someone calls claiming to be from Microsoft or BT, saying your computer has a virus. They want remote access to "fix it." Hang up — this is a scam. No legitimate company calls you unsolicited about your computer.

!

"You've won a prize!"

If you haven't entered a competition, you haven't won one. Links to claim prizes lead to scam sites.

How to Spot a Phishing Message

  • It creates a sense of urgency — "Act now! Your account will be closed in 24 hours!"
  • It asks for personal information — passwords, card numbers, National Insurance numbers
  • The email address looks slightly wrong — like noreply@amazon-secure.net instead of @amazon.co.uk
  • The language is poorly written — many scams come from overseas and contain grammatical errors
  • Links go to unfamiliar websites — hover your mouse over a link (without clicking) to see the real destination
🛑

The Golden Rule

When in doubt, don't click. If you receive a suspicious email claiming to be from your bank, close the email and go to your bank's website directly by typing the address yourself. Or ring the bank's official phone number (from their website or your card) to check.

Protecting Your Personal Information

Your personal information is valuable — treat it that way. Here's what to share carefully online:

  • Your full date of birth
  • Your home address
  • Your National Insurance number
  • Bank account or credit card numbers
  • Passport or driving licence numbers
  • Medical information

Legitimate websites and organisations will never ask for your full password via email. They may ask for some of this information on official, secure forms (like when setting up a bank account online) — but always check you're on the right website first.

Safe Online Shopping

Online shopping is convenient and safe — if you know what to check. Follow these simple guidelines:

  • Look for the padlock symbol (🔒) in the address bar and "https" at the start of the address — this means your information is encrypted
  • Stick to well-known retailers — Amazon, John Lewis, M&S, Boots, etc. If using an unfamiliar shop, search for reviews first
  • Use a credit card rather than a debit card for online purchases — you have more protection if something goes wrong
  • Never pay by bank transfer to an individual seller you don't know
  • Check the returns policy before buying
💳

Credit Card Protection

Under "Section 75" of the Consumer Credit Act, if you pay by credit card for something worth between £100 and £30,000 and it doesn't arrive or is faulty, you can claim a refund from your credit card company. This makes credit cards excellent for online purchases.

What to Do If Something Goes Wrong

Even with all precautions, if you think you've been scammed or clicked something suspicious:

1

Don't panic

A lot of suspicious links are harmless if you close them quickly without entering any information.

2

Change your passwords immediately

If you entered any login details on a suspicious site, change those passwords right away.

3

Contact your bank

If you shared any financial information, call your bank's fraud line immediately. The number is on the back of your debit or credit card.

4

Report it

In the UK, report scams to Action Fraud: 0300 123 2040, or at actionfraud.police.uk. You can also forward suspicious texts to 7726 (free).

Key Takeaways

  • ✓ Use long, unique passwords for each account — or a passphrase of random words
  • ✓ Legitimate organisations never ask for passwords or full card details by email or text
  • ✓ When in doubt about a message, delete it and contact the organisation directly using their official number
  • ✓ Shop only on sites with the padlock symbol (https) and with well-known retailers
  • ✓ If you suspect a scam, act quickly: change passwords, contact your bank, and report it
