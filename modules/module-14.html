Why AI Doesn't Read Like We Do

When you read text, you process it word by word (and sometimes phrase by phrase). Language models process text differently — they break it down into units called tokens before doing anything with it. Understanding tokens explains a surprising number of things about AI behaviour that otherwise seem mysterious.

This module goes into more technical depth than most — because these concepts are genuinely useful for getting the most from AI tools, and because they're more accessible than they first appear.

What Is a Token?

A token is the basic unit of text that a language model processes. It is not simply a word, though many common words are single tokens. A token is better understood as a commonly occurring sequence of characters — which in practice means:

  • Short common words are usually 1 token: the, cat, run, a, of
  • Longer or rarer words are often split into 2–4 tokens: un-believ-able, electro-magnet-ic
  • Punctuation and spaces are usually 1 token each
  • Numbers can vary: 42 might be 1 token; 2024 might be 1 or 2
  • Common short phrases or word endings are sometimes combined: -ing, -tion, pre-

As a rough rule of thumb: 1 token ≈ ¾ of an English word, or about 4 characters. A typical page of English prose (around 400 words) is roughly 500–600 tokens.

Why Tokenise Rather Than Use Whole Words?

The tokenisation approach (using sub-word pieces rather than whole words) was a deliberate design choice that solves several problems:

  • Handles unknown words gracefully. If the model was trained only on whole words, it would have no idea what to do with a word it had never seen. With sub-word tokenisation, it can decompose the new word into familiar pieces — un-usual-ness — and handle it reasonably well.
  • Manages vocabulary size. There are effectively unlimited possible words in any living language. Sub-word tokens cap the vocabulary at a manageable size (typically 50,000–100,000 tokens) that covers all text efficiently.
  • Efficiency. Processing 500 tokens per page is far more tractable than maintaining a vocabulary of millions of whole words and word forms.
🔤

Why AI Sometimes Struggles with Spelling

Here's an interesting side-effect of tokenisation: the model never sees individual letters directly, only token sequences. Asking an AI "how many 'r's are in 'strawberry'?" was famously problematic for early models — because the word is a single token or a few tokens, and the model has to reason about the internal letters of tokens it has learned as single units. More recent models handle this better, but it illustrates how differently AI processes text from how we do.

The Context Window — Working Memory

Every language model has a context window — the maximum number of tokens it can process at once. Everything inside the context window is what the model can "see" and reason about when generating its next response. Everything outside it is, in effect, forgotten.

The context window includes:

  • The system prompt (any standing instructions given to the model)
  • The entire conversation history so far
  • The current message you just sent
  • Any documents or text you've pasted in
  • The model's response as it's being generated
📋

The Whiteboard Analogy

Think of the context window as a whiteboard in a meeting room. There's only so much space. You write things on the board as the conversation progresses. When the board fills up, you have to erase older material to make room for new. The model can only see what's currently on the board — anything erased is gone from its awareness.

📄

What This Means in Practice

With 128,000 tokens, you could paste in an entire book and ask questions about it. For most everyday uses — writing letters, having conversations, explaining documents — you will never come close to the limit. Context windows matter most when processing very long documents.

What Happens When the Context Window Fills Up?

In a very long conversation, there comes a point where the entire history no longer fits in the context window. Different systems handle this differently:

  • Truncation — the oldest messages are simply dropped. The model doesn't know the early part of the conversation happened.
  • Summarisation — some systems automatically summarise older parts of the conversation before removing them, preserving the gist.
  • Hard stop — some interfaces simply warn you that the conversation is too long and ask you to start a new one.
⚠️

Practical Signs You've Hit the Limit

If the AI seems to "forget" something important from early in your conversation, or starts contradicting what it agreed to earlier, it may have lost those early messages from its context window. The simplest fix: start a new conversation and briefly recap the most important context at the beginning.

The Attention Fade Problem

Even within the context window, there's a subtler effect: models tend to pay more attention to the beginning and end of the context, and less to material in the middle. This is sometimes called the "lost in the middle" phenomenon.

Practical implication: if you're pasting in a long document and asking a specific question, put your question at the end of your message (after the document), and also state the most important context at the beginning. The model is more likely to attend to both ends.

Temperature — Creativity vs. Accuracy

When an AI generates text, at each step it doesn't simply pick the single most probable next token — it samples from a distribution. The temperature setting controls how wide that distribution is.

🧊

Low Temperature (≈ 0.0–0.3)

The model picks near-deterministically — choosing the highest probability tokens. Responses are consistent, predictable, and factual. Ask the same question twice and get nearly identical answers. Best for: factual questions, legal/medical information, code.

🔥

High Temperature (≈ 0.8–1.5)

The model samples more broadly from lower-probability options. Responses are varied, creative, and sometimes surprising. Ask the same question twice and get quite different answers. Best for: creative writing, brainstorming, generating alternatives.

🎲

The Dice Analogy

Imagine the AI choosing the next word by throwing a weighted dice. At low temperature, the dice is so heavily weighted toward the most probable word that it almost always lands there. At high temperature, the weighting is much flatter — the dice could plausibly land on many different options. Low temperature gives you consistency and precision. High temperature gives you variety and creativity. Both are useful, depending on what you need.

Do You Control Temperature Directly?

In consumer AI tools like ChatGPT or Claude, you typically don't set temperature manually. The tool sets it for you, usually at a moderate value suited for general conversation. Some interfaces let you choose between "Creative" and "Precise" modes — which is essentially adjusting temperature behind the scenes.

You can influence behaviour in your prompt instead:

  • For more creative output: "Be creative and surprising" / "Give me something unusual and unexpected"
  • For more precise output: "Be factual and precise" / "Stick to established information, no speculation" / "If you're not certain, say so"

Tokens and Cost

For users of consumer AI tools (ChatGPT free, Claude free, Copilot), tokens are largely invisible — you use the tool within usage limits without counting tokens yourself. But it's worth understanding the relationship for context:

  • AI providers price their services in tokens when selling to developers and businesses
  • As of 2025, typical costs for GPT-4o are around $0.005 per 1,000 input tokens — roughly a penny per 800 words of input
  • Output tokens (the AI's responses) are typically priced higher than input tokens
  • This explains why free-tier usage has limits, and why very long conversations may feel different from short ones
💰

For Everyday Personal Use

Free tiers on all major AI tools are generous enough for personal use. The vast majority of everyday tasks — writing letters, asking questions, having conversations — will comfortably fit within free limits. You would need to process very large volumes of text to approach meaningful cost considerations.

Putting It Together — Practical Implications

Knowing about tokens and context windows makes you a better AI user in concrete ways:

1

Long documents — paste them in

Modern context windows are large enough to hold entire contracts, medical letters, or reports. Paste the full text and ask the AI to summarise, explain, or highlight key points. You don't need to manually extract sections.

2

Start fresh for new topics

Rather than accumulating a very long conversation, start a new chat when you change subject. This keeps the context clean and ensures the AI's full attention is on what's relevant now.

3

Recap at the start of long sessions

If returning to a topic after days away, start a new conversation with a brief recap: "I'm continuing work on X from last week — the key context is Y." Don't assume the AI remembers previous sessions.

4

Position important instructions prominently

For complex tasks, put your most important instructions at both the start and end of a long prompt. Due to the "lost in the middle" effect, these positions receive the most attention.

5

Ask for precision when it matters

For factual questions, add phrases like "be precise and factual" to push the model toward lower-temperature behaviour. For creative tasks, "be creative and varied" opens up more interesting outputs.

Key Takeaways

  • ✓ A token is roughly ¾ of a word — common words are 1 token; long rare words can be 3–6 tokens
  • ✓ Sub-word tokenisation lets AI handle any text, including words it has never seen before
  • ✓ The context window is the AI's working memory — everything it can "see" at once
  • ✓ Modern context windows (128K–1M tokens) can hold entire novels — most everyday tasks never hit the limit
  • ✓ When context fills up, older content is lost — start fresh conversations when changing topics
  • ✓ Temperature controls creativity vs. consistency — you can influence this through your prompt wording
  • ✓ Models pay more attention to the beginning and end of long prompts — put key instructions in both places
