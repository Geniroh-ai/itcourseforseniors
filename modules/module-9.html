How AI Learns

Machine learning, neural networks, training data, fine-tuning — demystified. The ideas are genuinely fascinating once the jargon is cleared away.

🟣 AI Foundations ⏱ 18 minutes 📖 Reading + Concepts

What You'll Learn

  • The core idea behind machine learning — learning from examples rather than rules
  • How neural networks work — the architecture inspired by the brain
  • What training data is, and why its quality and diversity matters enormously
  • What "fine-tuning" means and how it shapes AI behaviour
  • How human feedback was used to make AI chatbots safer and more helpful (RLHF)
  • Why AI makes mistakes — and what specifically causes each type of error
  • The difference between "narrow AI" and more general systems

The Fundamental Shift: Rules vs. Examples

For most of computing history, programmers wrote explicit rules. Want to detect spam email? You'd write: "If the subject line contains 'FREE MONEY' or 'CLICK NOW', mark as spam." This works for obvious cases — but determined spammers simply avoided those phrases. The rules became an arms race, and the programmers were always a step behind.

Machine learning took a completely different approach. Instead of writing rules, you show the system thousands of examples — "this email is spam, this one isn't" — and let it figure out the patterns itself. The result is a system that can generalise to spam it has never seen before, because it has understood the underlying patterns rather than memorised a list of keywords.

👨‍⚕️

The Medical Analogy

A junior doctor learns from a textbook of rules: "If blood pressure is above X and symptom Y is present, consider condition Z." A senior consultant with thirty years of experience has seen thousands of patients, and her diagnosis draws on pattern recognition that she couldn't fully write down even if asked. She notices something subtle — a way a patient holds themselves, a particular combination of mild indicators — that the textbook never mentioned. Machine learning aims to build that second kind of knowledge, from exposure to vast numbers of examples.

Neural Networks — The Architecture That Changed Everything

The specific technique that powers modern AI is called a neural network, and the name is both accurate and slightly misleading. It was inspired by the structure of biological brains — but it is a mathematical abstraction, not a simulation of actual neurons.

The Basic Structure

A neural network is organised in layers. Imagine a factory assembly line with many stations. Raw materials go in one end, and a finished product comes out the other. Each station transforms what it receives before passing it on.

  • Input layer — receives the raw data. For an image, this might be the colour value of every pixel. For language, it's the words or parts of words.
  • Hidden layers — where the actual "thinking" happens. There can be dozens or hundreds of these. Each layer detects increasingly abstract patterns.
  • Output layer — produces the result. "This photo contains a cat." "The sentiment of this review is positive." "The next word is probably 'the'."

🔍 How Layers Build Up Understanding

Take image recognition as an example. Each layer learns something more complex:

Layer 1

Edges and Gradients

The first hidden layers detect very simple features — horizontal lines, vertical lines, diagonal edges, areas of colour contrast.

Layer 2

Shapes and Textures

Combinations of edges form circles, curves, and textures. The network starts to detect things like "fur-like texture" or "circular eye shape."

Layer 3

Parts of Objects

Combinations of shapes form recognisable parts — "an ear", "a snout", "a paw." Still not a whole animal, but components.

Layer N

The Full Concept

Deep layers combine all the parts into a confident recognition: "This is a cat" — even if it's a cartoon cat, a blurry cat, or a species of cat the network has never seen.

Connections and Weights

Every connection between nodes in adjacent layers has a weight — a number that says how much influence one node has on the next. When a network is first created, these weights are random. Training is the process of adjusting those weights until the network produces correct outputs.

Think of it like tuning a million-dial radio receiver, where you're trying to pick up a specific signal. At first everything is static. As you train, each dial gets gradually adjusted. After enough adjustments, the signal comes through clearly. A large language model has hundreds of billions of such dials.

Training — How the Network Learns

Training a neural network is an iterative process. It has three key steps that repeat millions of times:

1

Forward Pass — Make a Prediction

The network sees an example (say, a photo) and makes a prediction based on its current weights. Early in training, this prediction is essentially a guess.

2

Calculate the Error

The prediction is compared to the correct answer (which we know, because the training data is labelled). The difference between the prediction and the truth is called the loss or error.

3

Backpropagation — Adjust the Weights

A mathematical process called backpropagation traces the error backwards through the network and nudges each weight slightly in the direction that would have produced a better answer. This nudge is tiny — but repeated across billions of examples, it accumulates into genuine learning.

⛳

Golf and Feedback Loops

Imagine learning to putt in golf on a putting green. You hit the ball (forward pass). It misses left (error). You adjust your stance and aim slightly right for the next attempt (backpropagation). Over thousands of practice putts, your aim converges on something very accurate. The key ingredient is the feedback loop — knowing immediately whether you were right or wrong, and by how much. Neural network training is the same loop, but running millions of times per second on specialised computer chips.

What Training Data Actually Looks Like

For supervised learning (learning from labelled examples), training data comes in pairs: input and correct output. For example:

  • Photo of a dog → labelled "dog"
  • Email → labelled "spam" or "not spam"
  • Medical X-ray → labelled "shows tumour" or "clear"
  • Customer review → labelled "positive", "neutral", or "negative"
  • Sentence with a missing word → the correct missing word

For language models, the task is simpler to describe but enormous in scale: given a sequence of words, predict the next word. This is done on essentially all the text on the internet — billions of sentences, with the "correct answer" being whatever word actually came next in the original text.

Training Data — Quality, Quantity, and Diversity

The quality, quantity, and diversity of training data shapes the AI's capabilities, biases, and blind spots more than almost anything else. This is why data is sometimes called "the new oil."

Why Quantity Matters

More data generally means a more capable model. GPT-4 (the model behind ChatGPT) was trained on trillions of words — more text than any human could read in thousands of lifetimes. This exposure gives the model breadth: it has "seen" material on astronomy, cooking, law, medicine, history, poetry, programming, and virtually every other topic.

Why Quality Matters

A model trained on mostly poor-quality, inaccurate, or biased text will produce poor-quality, inaccurate, or biased outputs. AI researchers spend enormous effort curating and filtering training data. Even so, the internet contains misinformation, outdated facts, and human prejudices — and AI absorbs these.

Why Diversity Matters

Early facial recognition systems were famously poor at recognising women and people with darker skin tones. The reason was simple: the training datasets contained mostly photos of white men in professional settings. The AI learned what it was shown. Diversity of training data directly determines fairness and reliability across different use cases and populations.

⚖️

AI Inherits Human Biases

Because AI learns from human-generated data, it can reflect and amplify human biases present in that data. Research has shown that some AI systems have displayed biases in job screening, loan decisions, and medical diagnosis. This is an active area of research and regulation — and a good reason to treat AI outputs critically rather than as neutral truth.

Fine-Tuning — Specialising a General Model

Training a large language model from scratch costs tens of millions of pounds and months of computing time. So instead of building specialist AI from zero, the common approach is to take an already-trained general model and fine-tune it.

Fine-tuning means taking the base model and continuing to train it on a smaller, more specific dataset to adjust its behaviour. Examples:

  • A general language model fine-tuned on medical literature becomes a medical assistant
  • A general model fine-tuned on legal documents becomes a legal research tool
  • A general model fine-tuned on customer service conversations becomes a helpdesk chatbot
  • A general model fine-tuned to follow instructions becomes an assistant like ChatGPT
🎓

University vs. Specialist Training

Think of base model training as a general university education — you emerge with broad knowledge across many fields. Fine-tuning is like a postgraduate specialisation — you take that broad foundation and develop deep expertise in a specific area. The specialist training is faster and cheaper because it builds on what's already there.

RLHF — Teaching AI to Be Helpful and Safe

One of the most important innovations behind modern AI chatbots is a technique called Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF). The name sounds technical, but the concept is elegant and worth understanding.

A language model trained only on internet text will mimic whatever tone and content it saw in that text — including toxic content, offensive language, and confident misinformation. Early versions of these models were startlingly bad at being helpful in conversation. RLHF was the key breakthrough that transformed them into useful assistants.

How RLHF Works

1

Generate many responses

The model is shown a question and generates several different responses to it.

2

Human raters rank the responses

Real human evaluators read the responses and rank them: "Response A is better than Response B, which is better than Response C." They score for helpfulness, accuracy, safety, and tone.

3

Train a "reward model"

A second AI model is trained to predict what human raters would prefer — essentially learning human preferences from the ranking data.

4

Use the reward model to improve the main model

The main model is then trained to produce outputs that score highly according to the reward model — essentially learning to generate the kinds of responses humans prefer.

This process is repeated many times. The result is a model that is far more helpful, honest, and less likely to produce harmful outputs than a raw language model trained purely on internet text. ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini all use versions of this technique.

🤝

Why This Matters to You

RLHF is why modern AI assistants feel so different from simply typing into a search engine. They've been specifically trained to hold conversations, acknowledge uncertainty, decline harmful requests, and explain things helpfully. That training involved thousands of hours of human feedback from real people judging real responses.

Narrow AI vs. General AI

It's useful to understand that all current AI — including the most impressive systems — is what researchers call Narrow AI (or Artificial Narrow Intelligence, ANI). It is extraordinarily capable within its domain, but lacks the flexible general intelligence of a human.

🎯

Narrow AI (All current AI)

Excellent at a specific task or range of tasks. Chess AI beats every human. Image AI identifies cancer on X-rays better than radiologists. But ask the chess AI to caption a photo — it can't. These systems are not generally intelligent.

🧠

General AI (Hypothetical)

A system that could apply flexible human-like reasoning across any domain — learning a new skill from scratch, applying knowledge from one area to solve problems in another, having genuine understanding. This does not yet exist. Current AI is not "thinking" in this sense.

Large language models like ChatGPT feel surprisingly general — they can discuss cooking, history, medicine, and poetry in the same conversation. But this breadth comes from the vast range of text they trained on, not from true general intelligence. They are pattern-matching across a huge learned representation, not reasoning from first principles.

Why AI Makes the Specific Mistakes It Makes

Understanding the causes of AI errors helps you use these tools much more wisely:

!

Hallucination — Confident Fabrication

The model is trained to produce plausible next words. When it doesn't "know" something, it generates text that sounds like a confident, well-sourced answer rather than saying "I don't know." This is the most dangerous failure mode. Always verify facts from AI with authoritative sources.

!

Training Cut-Off

The model has no knowledge of events after its training data was collected. Ask about recent news, current prices, or last week's match result — it cannot know. Some versions have web browsing added; many don't.

⚠️

Distribution Shift

When you ask about something very unlike anything in the training data — a very niche topic, a very unusual phrasing, or a context the model rarely saw — it performs worse. It's operating outside its "comfort zone."

⚠️

Sycophancy

RLHF teaches models to generate responses humans rate highly — and humans sometimes prefer confident, agreeable responses over honest, hedged ones. This can make AI "people-pleasers" that tell you what you want to hear rather than what is most accurate.

ℹ️

Inherited Bias

Biases present in the training data appear in the model's outputs. AI companies actively work to reduce this, but it cannot be entirely eliminated because the training data reflects human society, which contains biases.

Key Takeaways

  • ✓ Machine learning learns from examples rather than rules — giving it the ability to generalise to new situations
  • ✓ Neural networks process data through many layers, each detecting increasingly abstract patterns
  • ✓ Training adjusts billions of internal weights by repeatedly comparing predictions against correct answers
  • ✓ Training data quality, quantity, and diversity directly determines the AI's capabilities and fairness
  • ✓ Fine-tuning takes a general model and specialises it cheaply for a specific domain
  • ✓ RLHF used human feedback to teach AI chatbots to be helpful, honest, and safe
  • ✓ All current AI is "narrow" — impressive at specific tasks but not generally intelligent
  • ✓ Hallucination, training cut-offs, sycophancy, and bias are the specific failure modes to watch for
