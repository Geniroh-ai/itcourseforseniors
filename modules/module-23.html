AI at the Cutting Edge of Science

Away from the chatbot headlines, AI is quietly producing some of the most significant scientific breakthroughs of the decade — solving a 50-year-old biology problem, decoding burnt scrolls from Pompeii, controlling fusion reactors, designing new medicines. A guided tour of the frontiers.

🟢 Real-World AI ⏱ 18 minutes 📖 Frontier Tour

What You'll See

  • The biology breakthrough that won the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry — and what it now lets researchers do in days that used to take years
  • How AI is accelerating the discovery of new medicines, new materials, and even better weather forecasts
  • How AI helped decode scrolls buried in volcanic ash for 2,000 years
  • How AI is helping to control nuclear fusion plasma and might help unlock clean energy
  • An honest picture of what AI does well in science — and what it still cannot do

The Biology Breakthrough — AlphaFold

Every living thing on Earth is built from proteins. These tiny molecular machines digest your food, carry oxygen in your blood, defend you against infection, and form the structure of your skin and hair. There are roughly 200 million different proteins known across all species.

For 50 years, one of biology's deepest problems was: given the recipe for a protein (its sequence of amino acids — written in DNA), can we predict the shape the protein will fold into in three dimensions? The shape determines what the protein does. A misfolded protein causes diseases including Alzheimer's and cystic fibrosis. Knowing the shape lets you design a drug that fits it like a key in a lock.

🧬

The 50-year problem

Before AI, working out the shape of a single protein took a team of crystallographers months or years of painstaking lab work. By the mid-2010s, we knew the shapes of about 170,000 proteins — a tiny fraction of those that exist. The pace of progress was glacial.

What Happened in 2020

DeepMind, a London-based AI lab owned by Google, released a system called AlphaFold 2. Given a protein sequence, it predicts the 3D structure in minutes, with accuracy comparable to lab experiments. By 2022, it had been used to predict the shapes of essentially every known protein from every known species — over 200 million structures, made freely available to researchers worldwide.

The reaction in the scientific community was disbelief, then awe. In 2024, the Nobel Prize in Chemistry was awarded to DeepMind's Demis Hassabis and John Jumper for AlphaFold, alongside David Baker for his related work on designing brand-new proteins. The first Nobel Prize given largely for an AI system.

🗺️

What it feels like for a researcher

Imagine being a geographer in 1500, charting the world one bay at a time, year after year — and then being handed a complete satellite map of the planet, free, downloadable. That is what AlphaFold did to biology. Every drug-discovery project, every disease investigation, every agricultural improvement project now starts with structures that used to be unattainable.

New Medicines, Designed by Computer

The most direct beneficiary of AlphaFold-style breakthroughs is drug discovery. A typical new drug takes 10–15 years and costs roughly $2 billion to bring to market, with about 90% of candidates failing somewhere along the way. AI is now shrinking parts of that pipeline dramatically:

💊

Insilico Medicine — fibrosis drug in trials

A Hong Kong–based company used AI to design an entirely new drug for idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (a serious lung disease). From "we don't know which protein to target" to a candidate in human trials took less than 30 months — a process that traditionally takes 7 years or more.

🦠

New antibiotics — fighting drug-resistant bacteria

Researchers at MIT used AI in 2023 to screen millions of chemical compounds and identified halicin — a brand-new antibiotic capable of killing some of the most dangerous drug-resistant bacteria on Earth. A 2024 follow-up paper found another class of antibiotics effective against MRSA. These are first wins in a fight that, before AI, was being slowly lost.

🧪

Designed-from-scratch proteins

David Baker's lab at the University of Washington (the third Nobel laureate in 2024) uses AI to design proteins that have never existed in nature — bespoke molecular machines tailored for a specific purpose. New vaccines, new enzymes for industrial processes, even proteins designed to break down environmental pollutants.

🧬

Personalised cancer vaccines

Several companies are now using AI to design cancer vaccines tailored to a specific patient's tumour — taking the genetic fingerprint of the tumour, predicting which fragments the immune system can attack, and manufacturing a custom vaccine within weeks. Early clinical trials are showing real promise, particularly in melanoma.

Weather and Climate — Faster, Cheaper, Better

Traditional weather forecasting is one of the most computationally expensive things humans do — massive supercomputers running fluid-dynamics equations across millions of grid cells over the atmosphere. A 10-day forecast takes hours of supercomputer time.

🌦️

GraphCast (Google DeepMind, 2023)

An AI weather model that produces a 10-day global forecast in one minute on a single computer — and beats the world's best traditional forecast on most measures. The European weather centre (ECMWF) and the UK Met Office are integrating AI models alongside their traditional ones. Better hurricane tracking, better heatwave warnings, better farming decisions.

🌪️

Earthquake aftershock prediction

Google and Harvard researchers built an AI that, after a major earthquake, predicts the locations of likely aftershocks more accurately than the previous gold-standard physics model. Used in California, Japan, and Türkiye.

🌍

Climate modelling

AI is being used to "downscale" big climate models to local resolutions, so a council in Cornwall can see what flooding looks like for their specific village under different warming scenarios. Better local planning, better insurance, better preparation.

Materials Science — The Periodic Table on Steroids

For most of history, finding a new material — a better battery, a more efficient solar panel, a stronger lightweight alloy — has been done by trial and error. Brilliant chemists with intuition and a lot of patience.

⚛️

GNoME (Google DeepMind, 2023)

An AI system that proposed 2.2 million new crystal structures and identified roughly 380,000 as stable enough to potentially be made in a lab. That's nearly an order of magnitude more candidate materials than the entire history of materials science before it. Researchers worldwide are now working through the catalogue.

🔋

Better batteries

Microsoft and the U.S. Pacific Northwest National Laboratory used AI in 2024 to screen 32 million candidate battery materials in 80 hours, then quickly synthesised one that uses roughly 70% less lithium than current designs. If it scales, this is potentially a very big deal for electric vehicles and the energy grid.

☀️

Better solar panels

AI-discovered "perovskite" materials are pushing solar panel efficiency past what was thought to be a theoretical ceiling just a few years ago.

Reading the Unreadable — The Vesuvius Scrolls

This one feels like fiction. In AD 79, Mount Vesuvius erupted and buried the Roman town of Herculaneum (next to Pompeii) under volcanic ash. In an aristocrat's villa, archaeologists later found a library — but the scrolls had been carbonised by the heat into fragile, blackened lumps of charcoal. Touch them and they crumble. For 250 years they were considered unreadable.

In 2023, three students entered a competition called the Vesuvius Challenge: take high-resolution X-ray scans of the scrolls (which can see the ink without unrolling them) and use AI to digitally unroll and read them. By 2024, the first scroll's contents had been substantially recovered — a previously unknown philosophical work, probably by the Epicurean philosopher Philodemus, discussing music, food, and the pleasures of life.

📜

An entire ancient library is opening

Hundreds more scrolls from the Herculaneum villa remain unread — and another room of the villa, untouched by archaeologists, may contain thousands more. Some scholars believe lost works of Aristotle, Sophocles, and dozens of other ancient authors could be hiding in those charcoal lumps. AI may be about to give us back a chunk of human civilisation that has been missing for 2,000 years.

Fusion Energy — Holding the Sun in a Bottle

Fusion — the reaction that powers the sun — would, if harnessed on Earth, give us essentially limitless clean energy with no long-lived radioactive waste and no risk of meltdown. Scientists have been working on it for 70 years. The trick is keeping plasma (super-hot gas) at hundreds of millions of degrees stable inside a magnetic "bottle." That stability is fiendishly hard.

In 2022, DeepMind worked with the Swiss Plasma Center to train an AI system to control the magnetic fields of a fusion reactor in real time — making millions of adjustments per second to keep the plasma stable. The AI achieved configurations that human engineers had never managed. Fusion isn't solved yet — but the path is shorter than it was, and AI is part of why.

Mathematics — Tools That Reason

In 2024 and 2025, Google DeepMind's AlphaProof and AlphaGeometry systems scored at or near the level of the world's top human competitors at the International Mathematical Olympiad — the most prestigious schoolage maths competition on Earth. Anthropic's Claude and OpenAI's models have independently been used to help mathematicians prove genuinely new theorems, working as remarkably patient research assistants.

This is significant because mathematics has historically been the area where AI has struggled most. That gap is closing.

What AI Still Cannot Do in Science

For balance — because the breathless press releases skip this:

⚠️

It does not actually understand the science

AlphaFold predicts shapes; it does not "understand" biology. The model has no theory of why proteins fold the way they do. This means it can be confidently wrong on cases that look unlike anything in its training data — and you wouldn't know without checking.

⚠️

It cannot run the lab

A new candidate drug or material has to be made and tested in a real laboratory. AI accelerates the "shortlist" step dramatically, but the wet-lab work, the animal studies, the clinical trials — those still take time and cost money. AI is a powerful research assistant, not a substitute for the rest of science.

⚠️

It does not generate truly new ideas (yet)

AI is brilliant at exploring vast spaces of possibilities much faster than humans. It is so far less good at the genuinely creative leap — asking a question no one has asked, or noticing a connection between two distant fields. Most "AI-discovered" results so far have been within the existing scientific paradigm, not paradigm-shifting in themselves.

⚠️

The risk of moving too fast

Faster drug candidates means more compounds reaching trials — and trials, by definition, are how we find out that something that looked promising in a computer is actually dangerous in a person. The faster the funnel runs, the more important careful late-stage testing becomes.

What All This Adds Up To

Across biology, medicine, climate, materials, archaeology, fusion, mathematics, and dozens of other fields not covered here (astronomy, particle physics, ecology, linguistics), AI is functioning as a kind of universal scientific multiplier. Researchers can ask bigger questions, explore wider possibility spaces, get answers in days that used to take years.

It is the least dramatic and arguably the most consequential use of AI in 2026. Most of these advances will not be felt by you directly for years — they'll show up as a new drug your GP prescribes in 2030, a more reliable hurricane forecast that saves a fishing fleet in 2028, a battery that makes electric cars cheaper in 2027, a lost play by Sophocles in 2031. The work is happening now, quietly, in labs across the world.

🌅

The honest hopeful note

For all the noise about chatbots and image generators, the place where AI is most plainly doing good for the world is in laboratories. Cancer treatments, weather warnings, clean energy, the recovery of lost human knowledge — these are the headlines that won't make the front page, but will, over the next decade, quietly add good years to many lives.

Key Takeaways

  • ✓ AlphaFold (2020) cracked a 50-year-old biology problem and predicted the shapes of essentially every known protein — awarded the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry
  • ✓ AI is now used to design new drugs (Insilico Medicine, halicin antibiotic) and personalised cancer vaccines
  • ✓ AI weather models (GraphCast) are matching or beating traditional supercomputer forecasts at a fraction of the cost
  • ✓ Materials science: GNoME proposed 380,000 new candidate materials; Microsoft used AI to find a battery using 70% less lithium
  • ✓ The Vesuvius Challenge used AI to read 2,000-year-old carbonised scrolls — recovering a lost work of philosophy, with thousands more to come
  • ✓ AI is helping control fusion plasma, edging closer to clean limitless energy
  • ✓ AI is now competitive with top humans at olympiad-level mathematics — a field where it had previously struggled
  • ✓ AI does not "understand" the science; it cannot run the lab; and the genuinely creative leap is still mostly human work
