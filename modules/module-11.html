How ChatGPT, Gemini, and Claude actually work — the architecture, the training, what makes them impressive, and where the limits lie.

What Makes an LLM Different

Before the current generation of AI, language systems were either rule-based (rigid and brittle) or statistical (better, but limited). Earlier chatbots worked by matching your question to a database of pre-written answers. If your question wasn't close to something already in the database, the system failed.

LLMs are fundamentally different. They don't match against templates. They generate responses — word by word, building each response from scratch based on everything they learned during training and everything you've told them in the current conversation. The result is a system that can handle genuinely novel questions it has never encountered before.

🗂️

Old Chatbots (Template-Based)

"Hello! How can I help?" triggers a lookup: do I have a response for "hello"? Yes — return response #47. Fixed, rigid, easily confused by unusual phrasing.

🧠

LLMs (Generative)

Generate a response from scratch, token by token, based on learned patterns across billions of documents. Can handle unlimited phrasings, follow-up questions, and genuinely new topics.

The Transformer — The Architecture That Changed Everything

The breakthrough that made modern LLMs possible was published in a 2017 paper titled "Attention Is All You Need" by researchers at Google. The architecture it described is called the Transformer, and it solved a problem that had stumped AI researchers for decades: how to process language with a sense of context.

The Context Problem

Consider this sentence: "The bank was steep and covered in grass." The word "bank" could mean a financial institution or a riverbank. Humans resolve this instantly using context — "steep" and "grass" tell us this is a riverbank. Earlier AI systems struggled with exactly this kind of contextual disambiguation across long passages.

Attention — The Key Innovation

The Transformer solved this through a mechanism called self-attention. When processing any word, the model simultaneously considers every other word in the context and assigns each a relevance weight — how much attention should this word pay to every other word?

For the word "bank" in our sentence, the model learns to pay high attention to "steep" and "grass" (which strongly signal a natural environment) and less to "the" and "was". This attention is learned during training and operates across the entire input simultaneously, not word by word.

🕵️

Reading a Room

Think about how a skilled detective processes a crime scene. They don't examine each object in isolation — they see everything at once and immediately notice which details are connected and relevant to each other. The bloodstain matters because of the footprints near it, which matter because of the unlocked window on the other side of the room. The Transformer's attention mechanism works similarly — it sees all the words simultaneously and learns which connections between them are most meaningful.

Why "Large"?

Early Transformers were modest in size. The breakthrough came from scaling them up enormously. Modern LLMs have hundreds of billions of parameters (the learned weights in the neural network). GPT-4 is estimated to have over a trillion parameters, though the exact figure is not published. Training these models requires thousands of specialised computer chips running for months.

Crucially, researchers discovered that as models were scaled up, they gained capabilities that weren't explicitly trained for. A model trained to predict the next word, at sufficient scale, spontaneously learned to do basic arithmetic, translate languages, write code, and reason through problems. This "emergent capability" phenomenon surprised even the researchers building these systems.

A First Look at Tokens

LLMs don't process text as words — they process it as tokens. A token is roughly a word, but it can be smaller (parts of long or unusual words) or sometimes larger (common short words or punctuation). Understanding tokens becomes very practical when you're using these tools, so they get their own dedicated module (Module 14). But here's the essential preview:

  • The word "cat" is one token
  • The word "unbelievable" might be three tokens: un · believ · able
  • A typical page of English text is roughly 400–600 tokens
  • The model processes everything — your questions and its own answers — as tokens
  • There is a maximum number of tokens the model can "hold in mind" at once — called the context window

Why does this matter? Because the context window determines how much of a conversation the model can consider at once. And because many AI services charge based on the number of tokens processed. Full details in Module 14.

The Three Stages of Building an LLM

Modern AI assistants like ChatGPT don't emerge from a single training run. They're built in three distinct stages, each with a different purpose:

1

Pre-Training — Building the Foundation

The model is trained on an enormous corpus of text (trillions of words from books, websites, Wikipedia, academic papers, and more) to predict the next token. This builds broad world knowledge. It takes months and costs tens of millions of pounds. The result is a powerful but raw model — knowledgeable but not yet helpful in conversation.

2

Fine-Tuning — Learning to Converse

The pre-trained model is further trained on examples of good conversations — questions and ideal answers, written by humans. This teaches it how to structure helpful responses, follow instructions, and engage in dialogue. The dataset is much smaller than pre-training data, but is carefully curated for quality.

3

Alignment — Making It Safe and Helpful

Using RLHF (covered in Module 9), human raters evaluate thousands of responses and train the model to prefer helpful, honest, and harmless outputs over unhelpful or harmful ones. This is why ChatGPT declines certain requests and acknowledges its limitations — behaviour that was deliberately trained in, not a natural consequence of the architecture.

Capabilities — An Honest Assessment

Modern LLMs are genuinely remarkable tools. Here is an honest, detailed picture of what they can do well:

Language and Communication

  • Writing at any register — from formal legal language to casual chat, from a personal letter to a press release
  • Editing and improvement — identifying weaknesses in text, suggesting rewrites, adjusting tone
  • Translation — competent in dozens of languages, though specialised human translators remain superior for nuanced professional work
  • Summarisation — condensing long documents to key points, with adjustable length and focus
  • Simplification — re-expressing complex technical or legal language in plain English

Knowledge and Analysis

  • Broad factual knowledge — draws on the vast range of its training data. Excellent for general knowledge, history, science, culture
  • Explanation — can explain complex topics at multiple levels of detail, adjusting to the audience
  • Comparison — comparing options, analysing trade-offs, laying out pros and cons
  • Research assistance — synthesising information on a topic into a coherent overview (though sources should always be verified)

Reasoning and Problem-Solving

  • Step-by-step reasoning — can work through problems methodically when asked to "think step by step"
  • Maths and logic — handles arithmetic, algebra, and logical reasoning reasonably well, though complex maths still benefits from verification
  • Planning — producing itineraries, schedules, to-do lists, project outlines
  • Brainstorming — generating many ideas quickly across virtually any domain

Creativity

  • Creative writing — stories, poems, scripts, song lyrics — across styles and genres
  • Ideation — suggesting names, concepts, approaches, angles
  • Roleplay and simulation — acting as a character, simulating a conversation, practising an interview

Limitations — An Equally Honest Assessment

The same systems that can write a compelling essay and explain quantum physics also have well-documented failure modes. Knowing them makes you a smarter user:

HIGH

Hallucination (Confident Fabrication)

The model generates plausible-sounding text even when it has no reliable information. It may invent book titles, fabricate statistics, attribute false quotes to real people, and describe events that never happened — all in confident, fluent prose. This is the most serious limitation. Treat all factual claims in AI output as requiring verification for anything important.

HIGH

Knowledge Cut-Off

Training data has a cut-off date. The model genuinely doesn't know about events after that date. Some versions have web browsing tools added — but the base model's knowledge is frozen at training time.

MED

No Real-Time Information

Without web access, current prices, live sports results, today's news, and live service statuses are unknown. Always use authoritative real-time sources for this kind of information.

MED

No Persistent Memory

Each new conversation typically starts fresh. The model has no memory of what you discussed last week. Some products are building "memory" features, but this is not universal.

MED

Sycophancy

The model was trained on human ratings and humans tend to rate agreeable responses highly. This means AI can sometimes tell you what you want to hear rather than the most honest answer. If you push back on a correct response, the model may capitulate rather than hold its ground.

LOW

Context Window Limits

In very long conversations, older parts of the conversation may "fall out" of the context window and be forgotten. Modern models have very large context windows (some handle entire novels), so this is rarely a practical problem for everyday use.

The Main Models Compared

The major LLM products each have distinct characters, shaped by their creators' values, technical choices, and business objectives.

🔍 Current Major LLMs (as of 2025)

GPT

ChatGPT / GPT-4 (OpenAI)

The model that launched the public AI moment. Extremely capable, versatile, and widely integrated into third-party products. Free tier (GPT-3.5) and paid tier (GPT-4o). Available at chat.openai.com. Also powers Microsoft Copilot.

GEM

Gemini (Google)

Google's flagship model family. Deeply integrated with Google Workspace (Docs, Gmail, Search). Gemini 1.5 and 2.0 have very large context windows (up to 1 million tokens) — excellent for processing long documents. Available at gemini.google.com.

CLO

Claude (Anthropic)

Created by a company founded with a specific focus on AI safety. Claude is known for being thoughtful, careful with claims, and particularly good at long documents and nuanced reasoning. Tends to be more cautious about expressing confident opinions. Available at claude.ai.

COP

Copilot (Microsoft)

Powered by OpenAI's models, deeply integrated into Windows 11, Microsoft 365 (Word, Excel, Outlook), and Bing. If you use a Windows computer or Microsoft Office, this is the most accessible entry point — it's built right in.

LLA

Llama (Meta / Open Source)

Meta (Facebook's parent company) has released the weights of their Llama models openly. This means developers can download and run them locally, without sending data to any company. Powers many third-party applications.

🎯

Which Should You Use?

For most people, the most accessible option is the best starting point. Windows users: try Copilot (press the Windows key and search "Copilot"). Google users: try Gemini at gemini.google.com. iPhone/iPad users: try Claude (claude.ai) — it works beautifully in a browser. All have free tiers and don't require technical setup.

How AI Companies Approach Safety and Ethics

This is worth understanding because it affects how different products behave. AI safety is a serious, active field of research — not a marketing term.

  • Anthropic (Claude) was founded specifically around AI safety research. Their Constitutional AI approach trains models to be helpful, harmless, and honest using a set of principles.
  • OpenAI (ChatGPT) publishes safety reports and has developed extensive content moderation. They've faced criticism for the pace of capability deployment vs. safety research.
  • Google (Gemini) has published AI principles and has a dedicated AI safety team, though their commercial pressures are significant.
  • Meta (Llama) takes an "open source" approach, arguing that transparency makes AI safer in the long run. Critics argue open source also removes guardrails.

None of these companies has "solved" AI safety — it remains an open problem. But being aware that these conversations are happening, and that different players have different incentives, helps you be an informed and critical user.

Key Takeaways

  • ✓ LLMs generate responses word-by-word rather than matching templates — enabling genuine flexibility
  • ✓ The Transformer architecture's "attention" mechanism lets models understand context across entire texts
  • ✓ LLMs process text as tokens — roughly word-sized chunks — with a maximum context window
  • ✓ Building an LLM involves three stages: pre-training, fine-tuning, and alignment (RLHF)
  • ✓ Hallucination, knowledge cut-offs, and sycophancy are the critical limitations to know
  • ✓ ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude, and Copilot all have free tiers — start with whichever is most accessible to you
  • ✓ AI safety is a real, serious field — different companies approach it with different philosophies
