How Chatbots Pay Attention

Every modern chatbot — ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Copilot — is built on one elegant idea: a trick called attention. Once you've seen it, the whole field stops feeling magical and starts feeling clever.

About this module

Second of three "Behind the Magic" deep-dives, inspired by MIT's introductory deep-learning course (6.S191). The actual mechanism inside ChatGPT is a few pages of mathematics; the idea behind that mathematics is something you've done every time you've searched YouTube.

The Ball and the Trajectory

Picture a still photograph of a ball, suspended in mid-air. Where does it go next? You cannot possibly know — it could be rising, falling, drifting sideways, struck from any direction. A single snapshot is not enough to predict anything.

Now suppose I show you three consecutive photographs: the ball low and to the left, then a bit higher and more central, then higher and to the right. The next position becomes almost obvious. The sequence — the order in which the snapshots arrive — is what unlocks the prediction.

⚾

Why this matters for chatbots

Language is exactly the same. A single word, on its own, tells you very little. The same word means different things in different places. "I'll book a table" and "the book on the table" share a word but not a meaning. To understand any sentence, you must read it in order — and you must remember what came earlier when you reach what comes later. That is the central problem of language AI.

Order Is Meaning

Here is a sentence that demonstrates the point starkly:

  • "The food was good, not bad at all." — a compliment.
  • "The food was bad, not good at all." — an insult.

Identical words, in a different order, and the meaning has reversed. An AI that simply weighed words like ingredients in a bag would have no chance of distinguishing the two. Whatever it does, it must respect which word came in which position, in relation to which other words.

Other obvious sequences: a song (notes in order make a tune; the same notes shuffled make noise), a heartbeat (rhythm matters), the stock market (today's price relative to yesterday's), the weather (a falling pressure means rain coming, a rising one means it clearing). Anything that unfolds in time is a sequence, and predicting what comes next means making sense of what came before.

The "Goldfish Memory" Problem

Before 2017, the standard approach was to march through a sentence one word at a time, carrying along a small internal "notebook" of what had been seen so far. By the time the AI reached the end of a long paragraph, it had to compress everything important from the start into that small notebook — and most of it had been forgotten or smudged.

Imagine being asked to read a 500-page novel aloud, one word at a time, while writing your understanding of the plot on a single index card. By page 400 your index card is hopelessly inadequate. That was AI's goldfish memory problem — and it meant chatbots of that era could not hold a real conversation, could not translate long passages without losing the thread, and could not answer a question that depended on something said many sentences earlier.

🐠

The cost of forgetfulness

This wasn't a small inconvenience — it was the wall that AI assistants kept running into. The cleverness was there in principle, but the model could not hold on to enough of the conversation to be genuinely useful. Then, in 2017, a small team of researchers at Google published a paper that changed the rules.

The YouTube Analogy — Attention Explained

Here is the single best way to picture what "attention" does. You already use it every day.

Imagine you go to YouTube and search for "deep learning." What happens behind the scenes?

🔎 Three things that meet

Q

Query — what you're looking for

The words you typed: "deep learning." This is your query.

K

Keys — labels on what's available

Every video in YouTube's library has a title — its key. "A beautiful sea turtle." "Kobe Bryant's best fadeaway." "Introduction to deep learning."

V

Values — the things themselves

Behind each title is the actual video — the value. What you ultimately want delivered.

YouTube compares your query against every key in its library and asks: how well does this title match what you typed? The sea turtle video is elegant, but it does not match. Kobe Bryant's fadeaway is brilliant, but it does not match either. "Introduction to deep learning" matches very well. So it returns the corresponding value — that video — at the top of your results.

🧠

And that is exactly what a chatbot does, on every word

Inside a modern chatbot, for every single word in your sentence, the system performs that same three-step dance — query, key, value. It compares every word to every other word and scores how relevant each is to each. The word "it" in the sentence "I picked up the cup but it was cracked" gets matched strongly against "cup" — because that's what "it" refers to. The whole sentence is understood by figuring out, for every word, which other words deserve the most attention.

Why It Was a Breakthrough

The original 2017 paper had a deliberately bold title: "Attention Is All You Need." The claim was that you didn't need the slow, word-by-word marching of older systems at all. Attention alone — done well, at scale — was sufficient.

Three things changed at once:

  1. The goldfish memory disappeared. The system can look at every word in your input at once and decide which matter most for any given task. Nothing has to be squeezed through a tiny notebook.
  2. Everything could happen in parallel. Instead of plodding through the sentence in order, modern chips can compute attention over all the words simultaneously. This turned out to be a dramatic speed-up, which meant much bigger models became feasible.
  3. The same idea generalised everywhere. The same trick works on words in a sentence, pixels in an image, notes in a piece of music, atoms in a protein. One architecture, many problems.
🏷️

The "T" in GPT

The architecture from that 2017 paper is called the Transformer. That is the "T" in GPT — the family that ChatGPT is built from. Claude, Gemini, Copilot, Mistral, Llama — every single one of these big chatbots is, underneath the branding, a transformer running this attention trick on a colossal scale.

Many Spotlights at Once

In practice, a single sentence has many different threads of meaning running through it at once. Take a film scene: there might be Iron Man in the foreground, an alien spaceship hovering in the sky, and a landmark building in the middle distance — all worth noticing, for different reasons. You don't pick one and ignore the others.

Modern chatbots use what's called multi-head attention — the model runs the attention process not once but many times in parallel, each "head" learning to focus on a different kind of relationship. One head might learn to track which pronouns refer to which people. Another might learn to spot when one clause is contradicting another. A third might learn to keep track of who is speaking. All these spotlights play across the same sentence at the same time, and their conclusions are combined.

🔦

Several readers, one page

Think of a book club where four readers each highlight a page in a different colour — one highlights characters, one highlights places, one highlights emotions, one highlights time references. When they compare notes, the whole structure of the page becomes vivid. That is multi-head attention.

The Same Idea, Everywhere

The most extraordinary thing about attention is how far it has spread beyond the chatbots that made it famous. Once you have a way to look at a whole collection of pieces and figure out which ones matter to which, you can apply it to almost anything:

📝

Language

Chatbots, translation, summarisation, email autocomplete, voice assistants. All transformers.

🖼️

Vision

"Vision transformers" now compete with the magnifying-glass approach from Module 16 for tasks like medical imaging and self-driving perception.

🧬

Biology

Proteins are sequences of amino acids; DNA is a sequence of bases. Transformer-based tools (DeepMind's AlphaFold among them) have predicted the 3D structure of essentially every known protein.

🎵

Music and audio

Music-generation tools, podcast transcription, voice cloning — all built on attention over sequences of sound.

🏥

Medicine

Predicting how patients will respond to treatments, analysing electronic health records as sequences of events.

Today: A Chatbot in Your Pocket

When ChatGPT first appeared in late 2022, the smallest version of the model still required serious cloud computers to run. By 2025–26, capable models had shrunk to the point where the same family of technology runs entirely offline, on a single phone, with no internet connection required. One MIT lecturer holds up a phone in airplane mode running an open-source model that scores better than the original ChatGPT on standard benchmarks. The fundamental engine — attention, learnt at scale — fits in your pocket.

💡

The takeaway you can carry around

The next time you type something into ChatGPT or Claude and it responds in a way that genuinely seems to understand you, picture what is actually happening: every word in your message is being compared to every other word, scored for relevance, and the most useful relationships are guiding the next word the system writes. It is not understanding the way you understand. It is a very elaborate, very disciplined spotlight — that is what attention is.

Key Takeaways

  • ✓ Language is a sequence — and the order of the words is most of the meaning
  • ✓ Older AI marched through sentences one word at a time and forgot the beginning by the time it reached the end
  • ✓ Attention is the YouTube-search trick applied inside a sentence: a query, keys, and values, scored against each other
  • ✓ The 2017 "Attention Is All You Need" paper introduced the Transformer — the architecture behind every modern chatbot
  • ✓ Multi-head attention runs several spotlights at once, each spotting a different kind of relationship
  • ✓ The same idea now reads proteins, medical records, images, and music — not just text
  • ✓ The engine that powers ChatGPT now fits on a single phone, with no internet connection needed
