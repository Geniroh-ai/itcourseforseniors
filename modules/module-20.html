What Is "the Cloud"?

Your photos sync to "the cloud." Your email lives in "the cloud." Your bank statements, your taxi receipts, your video calls — all somewhere in "the cloud." But where is that, exactly? And what does it cost you (in money, in privacy)?

🟠 The Bigger Picture ⏱ 15 minutes 📖 Concept Module

What You'll Learn

  • The one-sentence definition that demystifies "the cloud" forever
  • What a "data centre" actually is — and why three companies host most of the internet
  • How services like iCloud, Google Photos, and Dropbox work behind the scenes
  • What you actually pay (in money, ads, or attention) for "free" cloud services
  • Honest pros and cons — when the cloud is brilliant, and when it bites you

The Sentence That Unlocks It

Engineers have a slightly cynical line they like to repeat at parties:

💡

"The cloud is just someone else's computer."

That is genuinely the whole secret. There is nothing floating in the sky. When something is "in the cloud," it means it is stored on, and runs on, a computer that belongs to someone else — usually a large company like Apple, Google, Microsoft, Amazon, or Dropbox — sitting in one of their warehouses somewhere in the world. You access it over the internet (Module 19 explains how) whenever you need it.

That's it. The fluffy word "cloud" hides a very concrete reality: thousands upon thousands of ordinary computers, in giant buildings, working on your behalf.

Where the Cloud Actually Lives

Picture an aircraft hangar. Now picture it filled, floor to ceiling, with rack upon rack of humming computers — each rack the size of a fridge, each holding dozens of small flat machines called servers. Now imagine giant air conditioning units roaring along the walls to keep all those computers from overheating, and back-up diesel generators outside in case the electricity fails. Imagine a perimeter fence, security guards, and biometric doors.

That is a data centre. There are thousands of these buildings around the world. When you "save to iCloud" or "back up to Google Photos," your photographs are travelling — as postcards (Module 19) — to one of these warehouses, where they are written onto one of those servers. Often onto two or three of them at once, for safety.

🏬

A self-storage unit, but for files

Think of a cloud account like a self-storage unit in a vast facility. You drive your stuff over (upload), the staff put it in a locked room (your account), and any time you want it back you turn up and they hand it over. The storage company doesn't (usually) read your things, but they do hold them. The convenience is enormous; the trust required is also enormous.

The Three Companies Quietly Holding Up the Internet

Most people have heard of Apple, Google, and Microsoft. Fewer have heard of Amazon Web Services (AWS), Microsoft Azure, and Google Cloud. Together, these three companies run a staggering portion of the world's data centres — and rent them out to almost everyone else.

📦

Amazon Web Services (AWS)

Started in 2006 as a side project at Amazon, AWS is now the largest cloud provider on Earth. Netflix runs on AWS. The BBC's iPlayer runs on AWS. Vast chunks of the apps on your phone are renting space from Amazon. When AWS has a bad day — and it does, occasionally — large parts of the internet briefly stop working.

🪟

Microsoft Azure

Microsoft's cloud, second-largest, runs much of corporate computing — banks, hospitals, retailers, and government departments rent space here. Office 365 and Teams live in Azure.

🌈

Google Cloud

Google's offering — smaller, but powering all of Google's own services (Gmail, YouTube, Maps, Photos) plus many other companies. Notably strong in AI.

🌍

An unusual fact worth knowing

If those three companies all went offline at the same time — which, thankfully, is extremely unlikely — a startling share of the digital world would simply freeze. ChatGPT, Netflix, Spotify, your bank app, your supermarket's online ordering, your council's website, most video calls — many would stop working. This concentration is one of the more interesting quirks of how the modern internet has organised itself.

What "Living in the Cloud" Actually Means for You

Most of the apps you use every day are, in some way, "in the cloud." Here is what that means in practice:

Your Photos Are Everywhere at Once

When you take a photo on your iPhone and it appears, moments later, on your iPad and on your Mac — what happened? The photo was quietly uploaded to Apple's data centre (this is iCloud Photos), and then quietly downloaded again onto every other device signed in to your account. From your point of view, the picture is magically everywhere. From the network's point of view, there are several copies — one on each of your devices, plus the master copy in Apple's warehouse.

Your Email Doesn't Live on Your Phone

When you read an email in Gmail, the email itself is still sitting on a Google server. Your phone is showing you a copy. Delete it on your phone and the same delete happens on the server, which then propagates to your laptop and your tablet. This is why all your devices are always "in sync" — none of them holds the real version. The cloud does.

Software That Runs on Someone Else's Computer

Sometimes the cloud does even more than store your stuff — it actually does the work. When you type a document in Google Docs, the document itself lives on Google's server and your browser is just showing you a window into it. When you watch a Netflix show, you're streaming it from a server somewhere — there's no copy of the show on your TV. When you talk to ChatGPT, the chatbot itself is running on Microsoft's cloud (in a server farm in the United States or Europe), and your phone is just a remote control.

📺

The cloud-as-television idea

Old broadcast television worked by sending the same channel to every house — you couldn't pause, rewind, or pick what to watch. Now your TV streams from a server that holds every episode of every show. You're not really watching a "channel" — you're watching a private performance, sent from a warehouse, just for you. That is the cloud in action.

The Bill: Nothing Is Really Free

Some cloud services are paid for directly — Apple iCloud's bigger storage plans, Netflix's subscription, Microsoft 365. Others claim to be free — Google Photos, Gmail, Facebook, YouTube. It is worth being clear about who is paying for the data centres in each case.

💷

You pay with money

Subscriptions, storage upgrades, premium features. Apple, Dropbox, Microsoft 365, Netflix, Spotify Premium. The deal is straightforward: you pay, they hold your stuff and don't show you adverts.

👀

You pay with attention

The service is "free" but advertisers pay to put adverts in front of you. To target you precisely, the service studies what you watch, search, like, and click. Google, Facebook, Instagram, YouTube, TikTok. The product, in the famous saying, is you.

⚖️

What this means in practice

Neither model is automatically bad — both can be reasonable trades — but they are different trades. A paid email service like Apple Mail or Proton Mail doesn't read your messages to sell you adverts. A free email service like Gmail historically did exactly that (Gmail stopped in 2017, but the principle stands). Knowing which trade you've made with each service is part of being a thoughtful user of the modern internet.

When the Cloud Is Brilliant

✓

Your stuff survives accidents

Drop your phone in a swimming pool, and your photos are still safe — they've been backed up to a data centre. The replacement phone signs in and the photos reappear within hours.

✓

Everything works on every device

Start an email on your laptop, finish it on your phone, send it from your tablet. The cloud keeps all three in lockstep without you thinking about it.

✓

Heavy lifting happens elsewhere

Your phone could never run ChatGPT, or transcribe an hour-long voice memo, or generate a video, by itself. The cloud has computers vastly more powerful than yours — and they do the work, then send back the answer.

✓

Sharing becomes effortless

"Here's a link to the photos from the wedding." You're not really sending the photos — you're sending an address into the cloud. Anyone with the link can come and look.

When the Cloud Bites You

For balance, the honest downsides:

⚠️

No internet, no access

If your home internet is down and your photos are only in iCloud, you can't see them on your laptop. Anything stored only in the cloud depends on a working connection to retrieve.

⚠️

Account lock-outs can be devastating

If you forget your password and can't recover your iCloud or Google account, you may lose years of photos, emails, and contacts in one stroke. Set up recovery options (a second email, a trusted phone number, written-down backup codes) before you need them.

⚠️

Outages happen — and they cascade

When AWS or Azure has an outage, dozens of seemingly unrelated apps go down at once because they all rent space from the same provider. A 2024 AWS outage briefly took down banking apps, supermarket websites, and even some traffic lights.

⚠️

Privacy is a real consideration

Your photos, messages, and documents live on someone else's computer. The big providers encrypt the storage and are usually trustworthy custodians, but they are also subject to government warrants and (very occasionally) data breaches. For most things this is fine; for genuinely private records (medical, legal, financial), it is worth thinking about which provider you trust.

A Sensible, Quiet Rule of Thumb

For most people, the right approach to the cloud is:

  • Use it freely for everyday things — photos, contacts, calendars, email, documents. The convenience is real, the risks are small, and the cost (in money or attention) is usually fair.
  • Keep one local backup of anything irreplaceable — a copy of your photo library on an external hard drive once a year, just in case. Belt and braces.
  • Make sure account recovery is set up — for every cloud account that matters (iCloud, Google, Microsoft), check that you have a backup phone number, a recovery email, and ideally printed backup codes stored somewhere safe.
  • Be more thoughtful about deeply private things — your therapy notes, your tax records, your medical history. For these, ask yourself: am I comfortable with this living on a US company's server? Often the answer is yes; sometimes a local file on your own laptop is more appropriate.
🧭

The shift, in a sentence

For most of computing's history, your files lived on the device in front of you. For the last fifteen years, more and more of your digital life has quietly moved into warehouses owned by three or four companies. That isn't necessarily bad — but it is worth being aware of, so the convenience doesn't quietly become dependence.

Key Takeaways

  • ✓ "The cloud" is just someone else's computer — usually one of millions, in a warehouse called a data centre
  • ✓ Three companies (Amazon AWS, Microsoft Azure, Google Cloud) host the majority of the world's apps and websites
  • ✓ Cloud services keep your devices "in sync" by holding the master copy of your stuff centrally and giving every device a view of it
  • ✓ Heavy tasks like ChatGPT, video streaming, or photo backups run on cloud computers vastly more powerful than yours
  • ✓ You pay either with money (subscriptions) or with attention (adverts and targeting) — both are reasonable, but they're different trades
  • ✓ Cloud convenience is enormous, but: depends on internet, on account access, and on the provider not having a bad day
  • ✓ Sensible defaults: use freely for everyday things; keep a local backup of anything truly irreplaceable; set up account recovery before you need it
